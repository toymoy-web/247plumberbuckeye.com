Monsoon Season and Your Buckeye Plumbing: What Actually Breaks

By the Buckeye 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-10

Arizona's monsoon season, roughly mid-June through late September, brings sudden, heavy rain to a region that's built for the opposite. Drainage systems designed around a dry climate can get overwhelmed fast, and homes across Buckeye see a real, specific pattern of plumbing calls during these months that doesn't show up the rest of the year.

None of this is exotic or unpredictable once you know what to watch for. It's a handful of specific failure modes that repeat every summer, and most of them are far easier to prevent in May than to fix in the middle of an actual storm.

Main-Line Backups From Overloaded Drainage

When a storm dumps more water than area drainage can move, the excess has to go somewhere, and sometimes that's back up through a home's main sewer line. This shows up as multiple fixtures backing up at once, usually the lowest drains in the house first, and it's one of the more urgent monsoon-season calls we get.

A camera inspection can confirm whether the backup is purely a drainage-overload event or whether it's exposed an existing partial blockage that a normal rain event wouldn't have triggered on its own.

Outdoor Fixture and Irrigation Damage

High winds that often accompany monsoon storms can damage exposed hose bibs, irrigation risers, and outdoor plumbing that wasn't built to withstand direct impact from blown debris. A cracked outdoor fixture might not seem urgent, but a continuously running line can waste a significant amount of water before anyone notices, especially on an irrigation zone that isn't checked daily.

Water Intrusion Around Foundation Penetrations

Slab-on-grade homes have plumbing penetrations where pipes enter and exit the foundation, and heavy, sudden rain can occasionally find its way in through a penetration that's developed a small gap over time. This is more of a water-intrusion issue than a plumbing failure in the traditional sense, but it's worth having checked if you notice unexplained moisture near an exterior wall after a storm.

Why This Compounds With Buckeye's Existing Conditions

Monsoon-season stress doesn't happen in isolation. A drain line that's already narrowed by hard-water scale has less capacity to handle a sudden surge than a clean one would, which is part of why we see more main-line backup calls in neighborhoods with older plumbing during these months. Keeping drains genuinely clear going into monsoon season, rather than just functional day-to-day, measurably reduces the odds of a storm turning a minor issue into a backup.

What's Worth Doing Before the Season Starts

A main-line camera inspection or a preventive hydro jetting pass in late spring can catch a narrowing line before monsoon season tests it. It's also worth walking the exterior of your home and checking that hose bibs, irrigation risers, and any exposed outdoor plumbing look intact, since a small crack found in May is a simple fix, while the same crack discovered after a July storm often isn't.

What To Do If A Backup Happens Mid-Storm

If multiple drains start backing up during or right after a heavy storm, stop using water in the house entirely until it's cleared. Continuing to run water, especially anything going down a drain, just adds volume to a system that's already struggling to keep up. Avoid flushing toilets if a backup is affecting the lowest fixtures in the house, since that's often the first sign the main line itself is overwhelmed rather than a single fixture being clogged.

If the backup doesn't clear once the storm passes and drainage has had time to recover, that's the point to call rather than wait, since a lingering backup usually means there's a real blockage the storm exposed rather than a temporary overload that will resolve on its own.

A Seasonal Pattern Worth Tracking

If your home has had a monsoon-season drainage issue before, it's worth noting when it happened and what the weather looked like that day. A pattern of backups specifically during or right after heavy rain, rather than randomly throughout the year, points toward a drainage-capacity issue rather than a one-off clog, and that's useful information to share on any future service call.

How This Differs Across Buckeye, Goodyear, and Avondale

Monsoon exposure isn't identical across the three cities we serve. Avondale's proximity to the Gila River floodplain and the Tres Rios Wetlands area means its drainage infrastructure handles a genuinely higher volume during a major storm than a city farther from a floodplain. Buckeye and Goodyear's newer subdivisions, built with more current drainage standards, generally fare somewhat better structurally, but their sheer newness means there's less of a track record to know exactly how a given neighborhood's system performs under real monsoon stress.

Older sections of any of these three cities, regardless of which one, tend to have drainage infrastructure that predates current design standards, which is part of why established downtown cores see a disproportionate share of monsoon-season backup calls relative to newer construction nearby.

The Bigger Picture

None of this means monsoon season should be a source of dread every summer. Most homes get through it without incident, especially with drain lines that are already in reasonably good condition going in. The pattern we're describing here is specifically about the homes where an existing, partially narrowed line or an aging outdoor fixture meets a storm that pushes it past the point it could otherwise handle. Addressing the smaller issue before the season starts is a far easier conversation than an emergency call in the middle of a storm, both for your schedule and for what it ends up costing in the long run.
