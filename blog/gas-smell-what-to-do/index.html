Smell Gas in Your Buckeye Home? Here's What To Do, and Not Do

By the Buckeye 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-10

A gas leak is a genuine safety situation, not just an inconvenient repair. The correct response is straightforward, but it's worth knowing the actual sequence ahead of time rather than figuring it out in the moment.

None of the steps here are complicated, but the order matters, and a few of the common instincts, like flipping a light switch to see better, are exactly the wrong move in a real gas-leak situation.

What To Do Immediately

Leave the area right away, taking everyone in the home with you, and get to a safe distance outside. Don't stop to gather belongings. Once you're at a safe distance, call your gas utility's emergency line and a licensed plumber from a phone away from the house, not from inside it. Every second spent lingering inside adds unnecessary risk for no real benefit.

What Never To Do

Never operate light switches, either turning them on or off, since an electrical spark can ignite a gas leak. Never light a match, use a lighter, or create any open flame. Never use a phone, doorbell, or garage door opener inside the house once you suspect a leak, since even small electrical activations carry ignition risk in a genuine gas-leak situation. These precautions apply the moment you first notice the smell, not after you've confirmed anything further.

Never attempt to locate or fix the leak yourself. A licensed technician has the equipment to safely locate the source and the training to isolate it before repair, which is not something worth attempting as a first-timer during an active leak.

How To Tell If It's A Real Leak

Natural gas is odorless on its own; utilities add a distinct sulfur or rotten-egg smell specifically so leaks are noticeable. If you smell that specific odor, especially near an appliance, the meter, or a buried line's path in the yard, treat it as a real leak rather than waiting to see if it fades.

A hissing sound near a gas connection, dead vegetation in an unexplained pattern near a buried line, or a gas appliance that suddenly won't reach full flame or heat are additional signs worth taking seriously alongside the smell itself. None of these alone is definitive, but any of them combined with even a faint odor is worth a call rather than a wait-and-see approach.

What Happens When A Technician Arrives

We locate the source of the leak, isolate it, and repair the specific failure, whether that's a degraded flexible connector, a fitting, or a more significant line issue. Every repair ends with a pressure test to confirm the fix actually holds before gas flow is restored.

Preventing Future Leaks

Flexible gas connectors behind appliances have a real service life and are worth having checked periodically, especially on older installations. If you're adding a new gas appliance, having the line and connection assessed before installation catches sizing or capacity issues before they become a problem rather than after.

Why Buried Lines Are a Real Factor Here

The same desert clay and caliche soil that stresses water and sewer lines across Buckeye, Goodyear, and Avondale can also affect buried gas lines over many years of ground movement. It's a slower-moving risk than a failed connector behind an appliance, but it's part of why a periodic professional check, not just a reactive response to a smell, is worth considering for older properties.

What About Carbon Monoxide?

A gas leak and a carbon monoxide issue are related but different concerns. Carbon monoxide is odorless and comes from incomplete combustion in a gas appliance, not from a raw gas leak itself. A carbon monoxide detector is a separate, genuinely worthwhile safety device for any home with gas appliances, and it won't detect a raw gas leak the way the added sulfur smell does.

When To Call Even Without a Strong Smell

A faint gas smell that comes and goes, an appliance that's started acting unusual, or a gas bill that's climbed with no explained change in usage are all worth a call even without an obvious, strong odor. It's better to have a technician confirm nothing's wrong than to wait for a smell strong enough to be unmistakable.

What To Tell Family Members and Kids

If you have children old enough to understand a basic safety rule, it's worth explaining the sulfur smell specifically and what to do if they notice it: tell an adult immediately and don't touch any switches or open flames. A simple, memorable rule communicated calmly ahead of time is more useful than hoping a child instinctively knows the right response during an actual emergency.

Insurance and Documentation

If a gas leak causes any damage, whether from the leak itself or from a related failure, documenting the situation, including when you first noticed it and what you did, is worth doing for your records. This isn't the priority in the moment, but it's worth returning to once everyone is safe and the immediate situation is resolved.

The Same Response Applies Whether You Rent or Own

If you're a renter rather than a homeowner, the safety sequence is identical: leave immediately, call from a safe distance, and let your landlord or property manager know once you're safe. Don't wait for permission to leave, and don't assume someone else has already noticed or reported it.

The one thing worth remembering above everything else here: the smell itself is the signal, and every step after noticing it should move you away from the source, not toward investigating it further. Treat the odor as the only confirmation you need to act.
